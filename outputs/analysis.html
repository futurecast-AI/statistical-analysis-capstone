Study time and Portuguese final grades¶

Author: AI Foundations (Edward Hickman)

This analysis uses the real UCI Student Performance Portuguese-language dataset, collected through school reports and questionnaires at two schools. It asks whether ordered weekly study-time categories are associated with final grades, without training a predictive model or claiming causation.

Source: https://archive.ics.uci.edu/dataset/320/student+performance (Cortez, 2008; CC BY 4.0). The original CSV is beside this notebook. See ANALYSIS_PLAN.md for the analysis decisions and honest timing record.

Figure 1 reveals the grade distribution, including zero grades, but cannot show the study-time relationship. Figure 2 directly compares the ordered groups: grade centers generally rise, but distributions overlap substantially, and the highest-time group does not have the highest mean. 4. Primary hypothesis test¶

H0: The population Spearman rank correlation between weekly study-time category and final grade is zero (rho_s = 0). H1: The population Spearman rank correlation is not zero (rho_s ≠ 0). Use a two-sided test with alpha = .05.

Spearman correlation measures monotonic association and is appropriate for ordinal variables (Schober et al., 2018). Average ranks handle ties. SciPy's p-value is a large-sample approximation, not an exact probability; it assumes independent observations and suitable sampling. It is not the probability that H0 is true, nor a causal effect or a measure of practical importance. Inspect the coefficient and figures, not only the p-value.

5. School-adjusted sensitivity diagnostic¶

School-specific coefficients are descriptive checks, not new primary tests. For a secondary diagnostic, center the globally ranked variables within each school and correlate the residual ranks. Shuffle study-time ranks within schools 19,999 times (seed 20261119). The two-sided Monte Carlo p-value uses (b+1)/(m+1), never zero (Phipson & Smyth, 2010).

This diagnostic's null is no association under within-school exchangeability; it differs from the primary zero-rank-correlation null. Permutations hold school membership fixed but do not resolve classroom clustering, omitted confounding, or nonrandom sampling. With only two schools, this is not a cluster-robust population analysis.

def school_rank_permutation(frame, n_resamples=19999, seed=SEED):
    """Correlate school-centered global ranks and permute within schools."""
    x = stats.rankdata(frame["studytime"], method="average")
    y = stats.rankdata(frame["G3"], method="average")
    indices = [np.flatnonzero(frame["school"].to_numpy() == school)
               for school in sorted(frame["school"].unique())]
    for idx in indices:
        x[idx] -= x[idx].mean()
        y[idx] -= y[idx].mean()
    denominator = np.sqrt(np.dot(x, x) * np.dot(y, y))
    observed = float(np.dot(x, y) / denominator)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    extreme = 0
    for _ in range(n_resamples):
        numerator = sum(np.dot(rng.permutation(x[idx]), y[idx])
                        for idx in indices)
        extreme += abs(numerator / denominator) >= abs(observed) - 1e-12
    return {"rank_correlation": observed, "extreme_count": int(extreme),
            "n_resamples": n_resamples, "seed": seed,
            "pvalue": (extreme + 1) / (n_resamples + 1)}

6. Interpretation and limitations¶

The primary coefficient is positive but far from a perfect monotonic relationship, and Figure 2 shows substantial overlap. The small approximate p-value is evidence against the primary null only under the stated assumptions, not proof of causation or representative sampling. School adjustment is a sensitivity check, not a fix for every dependency. Self-reported broad time categories, prior attainment, family resources and possible classroom dependence can affect interpretation. Neither students nor schools were randomized; findings do not prescribe an ideal number of hours or describe all Portuguese students.

7. Notebook summary¶

I explored how ordered weekly study-time categories relate to final Portuguese grades in 649 student records. Grades generally increased across the study-time categories, but the highest-time group's mean was not the highest and the distributions overlapped substantially. The two-sided Spearman test found evidence of a positive monotonic association under its assumptions, not evidence of a causal effect. Important challenges were interpreting unequal-width ordinal categories, unequal group sizes, and possible dependence within two schools. I retained all records, documented the data checks, and used a school-adjusted sensitivity diagnostic rather than treating the pooled p-value as universally applicable.
